How to Use This Course

What this course is

This is a course on designing and governing Communications Data Acquisition (CDA) solutions, written from the point of view of someone who has to make, challenge and assure design decisions rather than someone who has to configure a network element.

It is framed against the UK Investigatory Powers Act 2016 as amended, and it takes the communications service provider (CSP) disclosure side and the interface and assurance between the CSP and the acquiring authority as its subject. The acquiring authority’s own internal tooling is referred to where it shapes the interface, but is not itself designed here.

It covers communications data only — the who, when, where and how of a communication. Content interception is a different power, a different legal test and a largely different design problem; the boundary between the two is drawn explicitly in Module 3 and then respected throughout, because in this domain a blurred boundary is a compliance failure, not a rounding error.

IMS appears because it is where a modern operator’s voice, video and messaging records are made. But IMS is treated here as a machine that produces data, not as a call-control system to be operated. The protocol detail that a telco engineer needs is compressed into Appendix C, where you can check a fact without it interrupting the argument.

An honest caveat

Law in this area changes, and it changed recently: the Investigatory Powers (Amendment) Act 2024 and the revised Codes of Practice brought into force during 2025 altered the notices regime, the internet connection record conditions, the bulk personal dataset regime and the section 11 offence. Every legal statement in this course is written as at September 2026 and is given so you know what to look up, not so you can rely on it. Before any statement here becomes a design constraint in a real programme, check it against the current Act, the current Code of Practice and your own legal advisers. This course is engineering guidance, not legal advice.

Part I — The Problem Domain

Module 1 — What CDA Is, and Why It Is Hard [C]

Objectives. You can define communications data acquisition and place it against the adjacent powers; you can name the parties and what each owes the others; and you can state the five structural tensions that make every CDA design a compromise.

1.1 The one-sentence definition

Communications data acquisition is the lawful obtaining, by a public authority from a telecommunications or postal operator, of data about a communication — who, when, where, how and with whom — but not what was said.

Every word in that sentence carries design consequences. Lawful means an authorisation regime and an audit obligation. From an operator means an interface between organisations with different incentives. About a communication means a data taxonomy with a legally sharp boundary. Not what was said means a filter that must be demonstrably effective.

Two observations that matter more than they look.

Parts 3 and 4 are a pair. Part 4 makes the data exist; Part 3 obtains it. A CSP-side design that treats them as one system will over-retain; a design that treats them as unrelated will find that the retained schema cannot answer the questions Part 3 asks. Most of the interesting design work is in the seam.

Part 9 is the design-forcing part. A data retention notice or a technical capability notice is where an operator’s abstract duty becomes a concrete engineering requirement with a delivery date. If you are governing a CDA programme inside a CSP, the notice is your requirements baseline, and the fact that it is a legal instrument rather than a requirements document is a permanent source of friction.

This is a legal act, and everything downstream inherits its scope.

4. The authorised request reaches the CSP — increasingly over a structured, machine-

Nine steps, two organisations, at least four systems, one legal instrument and one audit trail that has to survive inspection years later. That is the design problem.

1.5 The five structural tensions

Every CDA design is a negotiated position between these. Naming them makes design reviews far more productive, because most disagreements turn out to be about which tension is being prioritised rather than about the technology.

1. Completeness versus minimisation. The authority wants everything relevant; the law requires no more than is necessary and proportionate; the operator wants to hold as little as possible. The design decides this in concrete ways: what is retained, at what granularity, for how long, and what a query is permitted to return.

2. Latency versus deliberation. A threat-to-life request needs an answer in minutes. The same pipeline must also support a request that will be scrutinised in court in three years. Optimising for one degrades the other, and pretending one pipeline serves both is a common and expensive mistake.

3. Automation versus human judgement. Machine-to-machine interfaces are faster, more consistent and more auditable. But some decisions — is this within scope, is this content, is this third-party data — are judgement calls that regulators expect a person to have made. The design decides where the human sits, and that placement is a governance decision disguised as a workflow decision.

4. Evidential weight versus operational speed. Data that will be used as evidence needs provenance, integrity and a chain of custody that can be explained to a court. Data used only to direct an investigation needs none of that. Building everything to evidential standard is unaffordable; building nothing to it is negligent.

5. Capability versus exposure. Every capability built — every retained field, every query type, every interface — is also an attack surface, an insider risk and a political liability. The most secure CDA system is the one that cannot answer the question; the least useful one is the same system.

1.6 Why IMS makes it harder than it used to be

In a circuit-switched network, a call produced one record, in one place, with one identifier for each party, at the moment the call ended. That world is gone.

ThenNow
One CDR per call, from the switchRecords from several IMS nodes, at several stages, for one call
One identity: the phone numberIMPI, several IMPUs, SIP and TEL URIs, GRUUs, IMSI, IMEI, IP address and port
The network carried the serviceThe network may carry someone else’s service
A fixed location: the line, or the cellAn IP address that may be shared, carrier-graded, or a VPN exit
One operator end to endAccess operator, service operator, interconnect, and an OTT provider

That last row is the one most often missed in governance. Historically, communications data existed because operators needed it to bill. Modern tariffs do not require per-event records, so increasingly the only reason a record exists is that the law requires it. That changes the engineering: retention becomes a purpose-built system rather than a by-product, and its cost is a compliance cost with no commercial offset. Expect that argument in every business case you review.

Key takeaways

  • CDA is the lawful obtaining of data about communications; content is a different power with a different test, and the boundary is load-bearing.
  • Parts 3 and 4 of the IPA are a pair: one creates the data, the other obtains it. The design problem lives in the seam.
  • A notice under Part 9 is where legal duty becomes an engineering requirement — and it is your requirements baseline whether or not it reads like one.
  • Five tensions — completeness/minimisation, latency/deliberation, automation/judgement, evidence/speed, capability/exposure — explain most design disagreements.
  • Modern networks have broken the assumptions the old model relied on: one record, one identity, one operator, and a commercial reason to keep it.

Module 2 — The UK Legal Framework [C/G]

Objectives. You can describe the structure of the IPA 2016 as it bears on CDA, name the authorisation routes and who uses them, explain the four kinds of notice an operator may receive, and translate each legal concept into the design requirement it generates.

2.2 The acquisition power: Part 3

An authorisation permits a relevant public authority to obtain communications data where doing so is necessary for one of the statutory purposes and proportionate to what is sought to be achieved.

The statutory purposes include national security; preventing or detecting crime or preventing disorder; the economic well-being of the UK so far as relevant to national security; public safety; preventing death, injury or damage to health; and a small number of others including certain regulatory functions. Not every purpose is available to every authority, and not every purpose supports every data type.

Authorisation routes. Following the Data Retention and Acquisition Regulations 2018 — themselves a response to litigation about independence — the default is external authorisation:

RouteWho decidesTypical use
OCDAIndependent authorising officers acting for the IPCThe normal route for most authorities and most requests
Designated senior officerA senior officer within the authorityUrgent cases, threat to life, and certain limited categories
Judicial CommissionerJC approvalWhere the statute requires it, e.g. certain sensitive professions
Internal, intelligence agenciesAgency internal authorisation with IPC oversightNational security work by the agencies

The serious crime threshold. For events data acquired for the crime purpose, the crime must meet the serious-crime threshold. Entity data may be acquired for the broader crime purpose. This asymmetry is one of the most important facts in the whole course, because it means the entity/events distinction is not academic: it changes who may obtain what, on what grounds. Any design that cannot reliably classify a field as entity or events data has a legal problem, not a data-modelling problem.

Internet connection records. ICRs are a defined subset of events data — broadly, the record of which internet service a device connected to, and when, generated by the operator in the course of providing the service. They are subject to their own restrictions in section 62, including which authorities may obtain them and on what conditions. The 2024 Act relaxed one of those conditions to support “target discovery” — identifying an unknown person from a pattern of service usage — for the intelligence services and the NCA, with continued IPC oversight.

Governance note. ICR capability is the single most contested design area in CDA. If a programme you are governing is building it, expect the requirements to be ambiguous, the volumes to be badly estimated, and the political sensitivity to be high. Ask early and repeatedly: exactly which events constitute an ICR in our network, and who has signed that definition off?

2.3 The retention duty: Part 4

A data retention notice (DRN) may require an operator to retain specified communications data for up to twelve months. The notice is given by the Secretary of State and approved by a Judicial Commissioner, and it may specify the data, the period, the operator’s obligations as to integrity and security, and the arrangements for disclosure.

Key features that shape the design:

  • Retention is compelled, not permitted. The operator must retain what the notice specifies. It must not retain more than its other legal bases allow — data protection law still applies to everything else.
  • Retained data must be kept secure and to the same standard as the operator’s own business data, and must be destroyed at the end of the period unless another basis applies.
  • Retained data must be capable of disclosure without undue delay. That is a performance requirement, expressed legally, and it is the origin of most of the latency requirements in a retention design.
  • Notices are now subject to statutory review timelines following the 2024 Act and the 2025 notices regulations, where previously review could be open-ended.

2.4 Notices to operators: Part 9

Four instruments, and it is worth being able to tell them apart at a glance, because they generate very different work.

Three points about notification notices, since they are new and least understood:

  • They apply to operators above a customer threshold — set at more than 10,000 customers in the implementing regulations.
  • They require notification, not permission. The Secretary of State has no power under s.258A to approve or veto the change.
  • Routine security patching is excluded.

Governance note. A notification notice changes the governance of your own delivery lifecycle. Somebody must be able to tell, for every change going through your release process, whether it is notifiable. That is an architecture and impact-assessment capability, not a legal one, and if it is not designed deliberately it will be done by an overloaded human reading release notes. Put it on the design authority agenda before the first release, not after the first miss.

2.5 Oversight, and what it demands of the design

The Investigatory Powers Commissioner and IPCO inspect both authorities and, in respect of their obligations, operators. Inspection is evidence-based: inspectors look at records of what was requested, what was authorised, what was disclosed, and whether those three agree.

That produces a hard design requirement that is easy to underrate:

Every disclosure must be reconstructable. Years later, you must be able to show: the request as received, the authorisation it cited, the query actually executed, the data actually returned, who saw it, and when everything was destroyed. If your architecture cannot produce that narrative from its own records, it is not compliant regardless of how correctly it behaved at the time.

The Investigatory Powers Tribunal provides redress for individuals.

Its existence is

why error reporting matters: a material error — data disclosed that should not have been, or disclosed about the wrong person — is reportable, and the design should make errors detectable, not merely unlikely.

Key takeaways

  • Part 3 acquires, Part 4 retains, Part 9 compels capability. Know which one is generating the requirement in front of you.
  • The entity/events distinction determines the legal test, so it must be a first-class, enforced attribute of the data model — not a documentation exercise.

Module 3 — The Data Taxonomy [C/D]

Objectives. You can classify a field as entity data, events data or content and justify the classification; you can explain why the boundary is difficult in an IP network; and you can specify the controls a design needs so that the classification is enforced rather than merely asserted.

If Module 2 is the map, this is the compass. Almost every hard question in a CDA design reduces to “which side of a line is this data on?”

3.1 The three categories

+-----------------------------------------------------------+
|  CONTENT                                                   |
|  The meaning of the communication: what was said, written  |
|  or sent. Requires an interception warrant. NOT in scope.  |
+-----------------------------------------------------------+
|  EVENTS DATA                                               |
|  Data identifying or describing an EVENT on a system:      |
|  something happened, between these parties, at this time,  |
|  from this place, by this means.                           |
+-----------------------------------------------------------+
|  ENTITY DATA                                               |
|  Data about an ENTITY — a person, a device, an account —   |
|  and about the association between an entity and a         |
|  telecommunications service. Who holds this number?        |
+-----------------------------------------------------------+

Entity data answers who or what. The subscriber behind a number; the account behind a username; the device associated with a SIM; the address a service is installed at; which services an account holds. It is comparatively static, and it is the less privacy-intrusive category — which is why the law permits it to be obtained on a lower threshold.

Events data answers what happened, when. A call was made from A to B at this time for this duration; a device registered on the network at this cell; a message was sent; a data session started and stopped. It is dynamic, it accumulates, and in aggregate it reveals pattern of life. The law therefore attaches the higher threshold to it.

Content is what was communicated. For a voice call, the audio. For a message, the text. And — the part designs get wrong — anything that reveals the meaning of the communication, including elements of signalling data that would disclose what a service was being used for at a level of detail beyond identifying the service itself.

3.3 Where the content boundary actually bites

Three recurring cases. Each is a design decision dressed as a data question.

Granularity of a destination. “This device contacted example.com” identifies a service. “This device requested example.com/support/bereavement-benefits/apply” reveals the substance of the communication. Same protocol, same log source, different side of the line. The design consequence is that truncation must happen at generation, not at disclosure — if the full string is retained, it exists, and its existence is a risk and a disclosure target regardless of your intentions.

Signalling that carries meaning. Some signalling fields describe a service; others leak substance. A SIP header identifying the communication service in use is descriptive. A subject line in a message header, or free-text carried in a signalling field, is content sitting in a place that looks like metadata. Anywhere an operator retains a signalling record verbatim rather than parsing defined fields out of it, this risk exists.

Inference from aggregation. A single record may be unambiguously events data while a long enough sequence of them discloses far more than any one of them. The law does not generally reclassify data because it was aggregated, but proportionality and oversight expectations bite harder on bulk-like queries. The practical design answer is to constrain the query, not only the field: limit periods, require the request scope to be enforced mechanically, and separately log broad queries for review.

3.4 Design controls the taxonomy demands

The taxonomy is not achieved by writing it down. Four controls make it real.

1. A field-level data catalogue as a build artefact. Every retained field carries: its classification, the legal basis for holding it, the retention period, its source system, its known quality characteristics, and the date and owner of the classification decision. This is a machine- readable artefact used by the system, not a spreadsheet in a governance folder — if the catalogue and the schema can drift apart, they will.

2. Classification-driven access control. The authorisation type carried with a request determines which classifications may be returned. An entity-only authorisation must be mechanically incapable of returning events data.

This belongs at the query gateway,

enforced, not in a user interface that a trained operator is expected to use correctly.

3. Truncation and parsing at the point of generation. Content-bearing granularity is removed before the record is written to the retention store. This is the single highestvalue control in the whole architecture, because it converts a continuing risk into a one-off engineering decision.

4. A human review point with a defined remit. Somebody, with training and accreditation, looks at the result before it leaves — specifically for scope creep, content leakage and third-party data. The design decides when this is required: typically always for free-text or unparsed fields, and never for fully structured, pre-classified results. Making that rule explicit is better than making every disclosure wait for a human, which is what happens by default when nobody decides.

3.5 Third-party data

A request about A’s communications necessarily reveals something about B, who is not the subject of any authorisation. This is unavoidable and is accepted in the regime — but it has design consequences:

  • Minimise incidental identification. Return what the request needs, not the whole record because the whole record was easier to extract.
  • Flag it. Results containing third-party identifiers should be marked so that handling downstream is deliberate.
  • Do not enrich it. It is tempting to resolve the other party’s number to a subscriber name because you hold that mapping. Unless the authorisation covers it, that is a fresh disclosure about a person who is not the subject. This is one of the most common real-world errors and it is designed in, not operator error, when the tooling enriches by default.

Key takeaways

  • Three categories — entity, events, content — and the first two carry different legal thresholds, so classification has legal effect.
  • The hard cases are granularity, signalling that carries meaning, and inference from aggregation.
  • Classification must be enforced by the architecture: catalogue as a build artefact, scope resolution at the gateway, truncation at generation.
  • Constrain the query as well as the field; proportionality lives in scope, not only in schema.
  • Do not enrich third-party identifiers by default. Default-on enrichment is a designed-in error.

Part II — Where the Data Comes From

Module 4 — IMS as a Data-Producing Machine [C/D]

Objectives. You can explain what IMS is in two minutes to a non-specialist, name the nodes that generate communications data and what each one knows, describe the records a call or message actually produces, and identify where the data you need is not generated.

Appendix C holds the protocol detail. This module is only concerned with what IMS produces, where, and how trustworthy it is.

4.1 IMS in two minutes

The IP Multimedia Subsystem is the standardised system operators use to deliver voice, video and messaging over packet networks. It is what makes a 4G or 5G phone call a phone call rather than an app: it supplies the telephone number as an identity, interconnect to every other operator, emergency calling, supplementary services such as diversion and barring, lawful process, and charging that can be reconciled between operators.

Three facts are enough to reason about the data.

Fact 1 — signalling and media travel separately. The control messages that set up a call pass through the IMS core. The voice itself does not: it flows through the user plane, often bypassing the core entirely. This is why communications data and content are physically separable in IMS — a fortunate alignment between the architecture and the law, and the reason CD generation does not require touching media at all.

Fact 2 — the subscriber’s home network always sees the transaction. However far the user roams, the session passes through their home serving node. So the home operator holds a record of every session its subscribers originate or receive, which is what makes CSP-side retention viable.

Fact 3 — IMS is access-independent. The same core serves 4G, 5G, Wi-Fi calling and fixed broadband. One consequence for design: the IMS record tells you the identity and the event, but tells you comparatively little about where — location comes from the access network, and must be correlated in.

4.3 What one voice call actually produces

A single answered call between two of your subscribers, in an ordinary IMS network, typically produces records at these points:

t0   Caller's device already registered      -> registration record exists
                                                (P-CSCF, S-CSCF)
t1   Session invitation submitted            -> originating session record
                                                starts (S-CSCF)
t2   Originating service logic applied       -> service event if barring,
                                                diversion etc. apply (TAS)
t3   Destination resolved, routed            -> interconnect record if the
                                                call leaves (IBCF)
t4   Terminating service logic applied       -> service event (TAS)
t5   Called device alerted                   -> progress state
t6   Answered                                -> answer time recorded
t7   Media flows                             -> policy/QoS session record;
                                                NO content record
t8   Released                                -> release time, cause, duration
t9   Consolidated                            -> charging record(s) produced

So a single call yields several records from several systems, which must be correlated to produce the one row an investigator expects. That correlation is the central engineering problem of Module 8, and the thing most likely to be underestimated in a plan.

The equivalent for a message is shorter but has the same shape: a submit event, a routing event, a delivery event, and sometimes a delivery report — four records for one message, frequently in two different systems if SMS can travel either over IMS or over the legacy path.

Governance note. If a design reuses charging records, insist on two artefacts: a gap analysis against the notice, field by field, with the gaps accepted explicitly; and a change-coupling statement naming who must be consulted before a billing change goes live. Without both, the risk is real and nobody owns it.

Key takeaways

  • IMS separates signalling from media, so communications data can be generated without ever touching content — the architecture and the law agree here.
  • Different nodes know different things: S-CSCF for the authoritative session, P-CSCF for access and location, TAS for why a call behaved oddly, HSS for identity.
  • One call produces many records in many systems; correlation, not capture, is the hard part.
  • IMS cannot give continuous location, anything about OTT services, or content — say so early.
  • Reusing charging records is a legitimate first move and a dangerous end state; demand a gap analysis and a change-coupling statement.

Module 5 — Identifiers and the Resolution Problem [D]

Objectives. You can enumerate the identifiers in play and say what each is good for; you can explain why identity resolution is the dominant design risk in CDA; and you can specify a resolution design that is defensible under inspection.

An acquiring authority arrives with one identifier and a time. Everything the system does afterwards depends on turning that into the right set of records about the right person — and on knowing when it cannot.

Two structural facts drive most of the difficulty.

A person is not an identifier. One person may hold several MSISDNs, several IMPUs aliased to each, a tablet with its own IMPI, and a Wi-Fi-calling registration from a device with no SIM at all. Conversely one MSISDN may have been held by three people over eighteen months.

Identifiers are reused. Numbers are recycled after cessation. IP addresses are reassigned continuously. IMEIs are cloned. Therefore every identifier lookup is only valid with respect to a time, and a design that resolves an identifier without a temporal qualifier is producing confident answers about the wrong people. This is the single most consequential correctness requirement in the whole system.

5.2 The three resolution problems

(A) Identifier  ->  Subscriber          "who holds this number?"
    Entity data. Needs: time-qualified account history.

(B) Identifier  ->  Other identifiers    "what else is this person?"
    Entity data. Needs: binding history across MSISDN/IMSI/IMEI/IMPU.
    DANGER: over-expansion. Each hop widens the disclosure.

(C) Identifier + time  ->  Events        "what did they do?"
    Events data. Needs: correct resolution at (A)/(B) FIRST,
    then a scoped query.

Problem (B) is where designs go wrong in a way that matters. It is technically easy to walk the graph — this MSISDN was in this device, which also held that SIM, which is registered to that account — and each hop feels like the same enquiry. Legally, each hop may be a disclosure about a different person, and the authorisation may not cover it.

Governance note. Require the design to answer: how many hops does a resolution perform, is that number bounded, is the bound configurable per authorisation, and is the hop count in the audit record? If the answer is “the tool follows the links”, the design has delegated a legal judgement to a graph traversal.

5.3 Carrier-grade NAT, and why it breaks things

The IP-address-to-subscriber lookup is the case that most often fails in practice, and the reason is address sharing.

Subscriber A  10.x.x.x:...  \
Subscriber B  10.x.x.x:...   >-- CGNAT --> 203.0.113.7 : port ranges
Subscriber C  10.x.x.x:...  /
                                one public address, hundreds of subscribers

Given only a public IP address and a timestamp, the operator cannot say which subscriber it was. Resolution requires, at minimum, the source port and an accurate time, matched against the NAT translation records for that instant.

That produces three hard design

requirements:

1. Port must be part of the request. If the acquiring authority’s source system does not

capture source port, the enquiry is unanswerable and the design should return a clear “insufficient information” rather than a best guess. Getting this stated in the interface specification, rather than discovered case by case, saves an enormous amount of friction.

2. Clock accuracy becomes a compliance control. Translation bindings last seconds.

A clock skew of a few seconds between the authority’s source, the operator’s NAT logs and the retention store turns a correct answer into a wrong one. Time synchronisation is therefore not an infrastructure detail in this system; it is a correctness control that belongs in the assurance regime with evidence attached.

3. NAT logging volume is the dominant capacity driver. Translation records vastly

outnumber session records. Any capacity model that does not size this separately is wrong, usually by an order of magnitude.

Deterministic NAT allocation — giving each subscriber a fixed port range — reduces log volume by orders of magnitude at some cost in address efficiency. It is one of the highestleverage design decisions available and is frequently made by the IP network team without reference to the CDA programme.

5.4 Designing resolution defensibly

Model bindings as intervals, not as facts. Every association — MSISDN-to-account, IMSI- to-MSISDN, IP-to-subscriber — is valid from a time to a time. The primary key of the resolution store is (identifier, type, valid-from, valid-to). Queries take a time and select the binding in force. This sounds obvious and is very often not what the underlying business systems provide, because they hold current state and overwrite history.

Result MUST distinguish:
  - 01 Feb .. 14 Feb : account A
  - 15 Feb .. 19 Feb : NOT ALLOCATED   <- say so explicitly
  - 20 Feb .. 28 Feb : account B       <- a different person!

That third row is the one that produces wrongful disclosure if the design returns “account B” for the whole period. The requirement is that a resolution returns a time-segmented answer, including explicit gaps, not a single value.

Return confidence, not just values.

A resolution can be exact (a unique binding),

ambiguous (several candidates, e.g. CGNAT without port), or absent. The interface must be able to express all three. Collapsing ambiguity into a single best answer is an error the receiving analyst cannot detect.

Separate resolution from retrieval. Resolution is an entity-data operation; retrieval of events is an events-data operation with a higher threshold. Keeping them as distinct services with distinct authorisation checks makes the legal boundary enforceable and the audit legible. Fusing them into one “search” is convenient and makes the entity/events distinction impossible to demonstrate.

5.5 Number portability and interconnect

Two more sources of wrong answers, both worth a governance question.

Portability. A number no longer identifies an operator. A request arriving at the wrong operator should produce a clear, standard “not our subscriber” response rather than an empty result — an empty result is indistinguishable from “no activity”, which is a materially different fact. Getting that distinction into the interface specification is a small change with large downstream value.

Interconnect. When the other party is on another network, you hold their number but nothing about them. Inter-operator identifiers in the records identify the peer operator, which tells the authority where to send the next enquiry. Retaining that field is cheap and disproportionately useful; it is also frequently dropped during pipeline design because it looks like routing minutiae.

Key takeaways

  • Every identifier lookup is only valid with respect to a time; bindings are intervals, not facts, and the answer must be time-segmented with explicit gaps.
  • Identifier-to-identifier expansion is a legal act per hop, not a graph traversal. Bound it, configure it per authorisation, and audit the hop count.
  • CGNAT makes IP resolution impossible without source port and accurate time; say so in the interface specification and return “insufficient information” rather than a guess.
  • Deterministic NAT allocation is a high-leverage decision usually taken outside the programme. Go and find out what was decided.
  • Distinguish “not our subscriber” from “no activity”, and keep resolution and retrieval as separate, separately authorised services.

Module 6 — What the Data Can and Cannot Support [C/G]

Objectives. You can set realistic expectations with an acquiring authority; you can explain the structural erosion of communications data and what it means for a ten-year investment; and you can frame the honest limitations of the capability in a way a governance board can act on.

This module exists because the commonest failure in CDA programmes is not technical. It is a requirement accepted that the data could never satisfy.

6.1 The four honest limits

1. The data describes the service, not the person. A record says that an identity used a service at a time. It does not say who was holding the device, whether the SIM was in its registered owner’s phone, or whether the account holder was in the country. Every inference from identity to person is exactly that — an inference — and the CSP’s disclosure should not present it as more.

2. Absence of a record is weak evidence of absence of an event. Records are missing for many innocent reasons: a node was restarted, a feed was late, a record failed validation, the service was carried by a path that does not generate a record, or the event was of a type never retained. A design must be able to distinguish “we hold no record” from “we can confirm nothing happened”, and must communicate that difference. Most cannot, and the gap is invisible to the recipient.

3. Timestamps are approximate and heterogeneous. Different nodes stamp at different points in a transaction, with different clocks and different conventions about what “start” means. Sub-second precision in a record is almost never sub-second accuracy across sources. Where timing matters — and in CGNAT resolution it decisively does — the design must state its accuracy, not its precision.

4. Location is coarse, occasional and inferred. IMS-era location is cell or access-point granularity, captured at the moments when signalling happens. It is not a track. Presenting a sequence of such points as a movement history invites over-interpretation, and the design can mitigate that by disclosing the uncertainty alongside the value.

Governance note. These four limits belong in a written capability statement that the CSP maintains and the acquiring authority acknowledges. It is the artefact that prevents a slow drift into expectations the system cannot meet, and it is also, if an error ever reaches the Tribunal, the document that shows the operator described its capability honestly.

6.2 The erosion problem

The long-run trend is that the proportion of communications visible to a network operator falls. This is the single most important fact for anyone making a ten-year investment decision in this area.

The drivers:

  • Over-the-top services. A message sent through a third-party application is, to the access operator, an encrypted data session to a cloud provider. The operator can say a session occurred; it cannot say to whom the user was speaking. That information, if it exists, sits with the application provider, who is frequently outside the jurisdiction.
  • Encryption in transport. Encrypted DNS, encrypted SNI and QUIC progressively remove the fields that once let an operator identify the service being contacted, which is exactly the data that ICRs depend on.
  • Address sharing and privacy infrastructure. CGNAT, VPNs, private relay services and IPv6 privacy addressing all weaken the identifier-to-subscriber link.
  • Flat-rate tariffs. As Module 4 noted, the commercial reason to generate per-event records has gone, so records increasingly exist only because they are compelled.

The governance consequence is not that the capability is pointless — the data that remains is high value, particularly identity resolution and the service usage the operator does carry. It is that a business case which assumes constant or growing evidential yield is wrong, and that the right architectural posture is modularity: assume the sources change, and do not couple the retention schema, the query model and the handover interface so tightly that replacing a source means rebuilding the system.

6.3 What remains valuable, and is often under-exploited

Against that erosion, four things get more valuable, and they are worth naming because programmes tend to spend disproportionately on the eroding half.

6.4 Setting expectations in practice

Three artefacts do most of the work, and all three are cheap relative to their effect.

A data availability matrix. For each request type, what the operator can return, at what granularity, for what historical period, and with what known caveats. Published to the acquiring authority and version-controlled.

A defined “nil return” vocabulary. Distinct, documented responses for: not our subscriber; identifier not allocated in this period; no records held for this period; insufficient information to resolve; request outside authorised scope. Five responses, five different meanings, and collapsing them into “nothing found” destroys information the recipient needs.

A caveat that travels with the data. Every disclosure carries its own provenance and limitation statement, so that the caveat cannot be separated from the result as it moves through the recipient’s systems.

Key takeaways

  • The data describes the service, not the person; absence of a record is not absence of an event; timestamps are approximate; location is occasional.
  • Operator-visible communications data is structurally eroding.

Design for modular replacement of sources, and do not build a business case that assumes constant yield.

  • Identity resolution, attachment events and operator-run service records are the parts that hold their value.
  • Three cheap artefacts — availability matrix, nil-return vocabulary, travelling caveats — prevent most expectation failures.

Governance note. When reviewing a vendor architecture, map it onto these six layers and ask where each control lives. Products frequently merge L4 and L5 — a single “investigation workbench” — which puts scope enforcement and result assembly in the same component as the user interface. That is not automatically wrong, but it means scope is enforced by the application the user is driving rather than by a service beneath them, and the audit story changes accordingly.

7.2 Layer responsibilities in detail

L1 — Sources. Not built by the programme, but constrained by it. The key governance act here is establishing, per source, a data supply agreement: what is produced, at what completeness, with what timing, and who must be consulted before it changes. Without this, a routine network upgrade silently changes a retained field’s meaning.

L2 — Ingest and normalisation. The most underestimated layer. It performs:

  • parsing of source records into defined fields;
  • truncation of content-bearing granularity — before storage, irreversibly;
  • classification of every field against the catalogue;
  • normalisation of identifiers into canonical forms (E.164, URI forms);
  • time normalisation to a single reference with recorded source accuracy;
  • correlation of the several records for one event into a correlated set;
  • validation, with rejected records counted, reported and retained separately for diagnosis.

L3 — Retention store. Module 8.

L4 — Query and resolution. Two distinct services, deliberately:

Entity Resolution Service        Events Retrieval Service
------------------------        ------------------------
input: identifier + period      input: resolved identity set + period
                                       + authorised field classes
output: time-segmented          output: scoped record set
        bindings + confidence           + completeness statement
threshold: entity               threshold: events (higher)

L5 — Disclosure management. The workflow layer, and the place where the human sits. Responsibilities: validating that the authority cited is present, current and covers what is asked; resolving the authorised scope; dispatching to L4; applying the review rule; assembling the response with provenance and caveats; recording everything; releasing.

L6 — External interface. Module 9.

L0 — Cross-cutting. Audit, time, access control, keys, monitoring and change control. Treated as a layer because in this domain they are functional requirements with legal force, not non-functional afterthoughts.

7.3 The three decisions that determine longevity

Most architectural choices in this domain are recoverable. Three are not, and they deserve explicit design-authority decisions with recorded rationale.

Decision 1 — Where is content-bearing granularity removed?

OptionConsequence
At generation, in the sourceCleanest. The data never exists. Requires network-element capability and cooperation; may be impossible with COTS elements.
At ingest (L2), before storageThe standard answer. The full value exists transiently in the pipeline, so the pipeline itself becomes a controlled environment.
At disclosure (L5)Weakest. The content-bearing value is retained for months and is a standing risk and disclosure target. Avoid; if forced, document why and compensate with access controls.

Decision 2 — Is retention a copy or the system of record?

OptionBuys youCosts you
Dedicated retention store, fed from productionIsolation; independent lifecycle; classification by design; production changes do not directly break complianceDuplication; reconciliation burden; cost
Query production systems on demandNo duplication; always currentProduction performance risk; no independent retention control; deletion cannot be guaranteed; change coupling

Practically, the dedicated store wins for anything under a retention notice. The interesting variant is hybrid: a dedicated store for compelled retention, with on-demand access to production for data held on other bases. That hybrid is defensible but requires the boundary to be explicit, because the two halves have different legal bases and different permitted uses.

Decision 3 — How is scope enforced?

OptionAssurance strength
Procedurally — trained staff apply the authorisationWeakest. Depends on human consistency; hard to evidence; fails under volume
In the application — UI restricts what can be askedModerate. Bypassable by anyone with data-layer access
In a scope-resolution service beneath the applicationStrong. Every query carries a machine-readable authorisation; the service intersects and enforces; the audit records requested/authorised/returned
In the data layer — row and column security bound to the authorisationStrongest, hardest to build, least flexible

Governance note. This is the decision that most determines whether an inspection goes well. “We trained our staff” is a materially weaker answer than “the system could not have returned that, and here is the log of the intersection it computed.”

  • Six layers, five boundaries, and each boundary carries a named control. Collapsing a boundary removes a control.
  • Ingest and normalisation is the layer most often underestimated and the one where the irreversible content decision is taken.
  • Keep entity resolution and events retrieval as separate, separately authorised services; that is what makes the legal boundary demonstrable.
  • Three non-recoverable decisions: where truncation happens, whether retention is a copy or the system of record, and how scope is enforced.
  • Machine determines scope from the authorisation; human approves release.

Design for partial correlation.

Some contributing records will be late, missing or

unmatched. The store should hold the correlated record with a completeness indicator per source, rather than waiting for perfection or discarding the fragment. That indicator then travels into the disclosure as part of the caveat, which is exactly the honesty Module 6 demanded.

8.3 Data model shape

Three families, with different characteristics and, usefully, different retention arguments:

Keeping them physically separate is worth doing for four reasons: they have different volumes by orders of magnitude; different retention periods are often justified; the entity/events access control boundary becomes a physical boundary rather than a logical one; and the resolution aids can be sized, tuned and — if the law allows — expired independently.

Every record carries: its classification, its source, its ingestion time, its expiry time, and a reference sufficient to reconstruct its derivation. That last field is what makes the store auditable rather than merely queryable.

8.4 Partitioning, expiry and deletion

Time partitioning is not an optimisation here; it is the deletion mechanism.

Three refinements that separate a good design from a compliant-looking one:

  • Deletion produces evidence. A deletion job that leaves no artefact cannot be inspected. The evidence record — what was deleted, how many, when, verified how — must itself be retained longer than the data, because it is the proof that the obligation was met.
  • Legal hold is a designed feature, not an exception. Where data is subject to ongoing proceedings, it must be exemptable from expiry, and that exemption must be authorised, time-bounded and reviewed. Designs that lack this end up suspending the deletion job, which suspends it for everything.
  • Crypto-shredding as the practical answer to backups. Where per-partition keys are used, destroying the key makes the data unrecoverable everywhere it has been copied. This is usually the only workable answer to the backup problem, and it drives a key management design with real obligations.

8.5 Capacity — and how to challenge a plan

Five drivers, in descending order of how often they are underestimated:

1. NAT / translation records.

Typically the largest by far. Sized by sessions per subscriber per day, not by calls. Deterministic allocation changes the number by orders of magnitude.

2. Registration and attachment events. Devices re-register more often than people

imagine, and IoT populations behave nothing like handsets.

3. Messaging events. Including delivery reports, which double the count. 4. Correlated session records. The one everyone sizes, and usually the smallest. 5. Indexes. Frequently as large as the data, and often omitted entirely from the plan.

Questions that expose a weak capacity plan:

  • What is the assumed ratio of translation records to session records, and where did it come from — measurement or assumption?
  • Does the model include indexes, replicas and the correlated copy, or only raw record volume?
  • What happens at the 12-month boundary — is steady state modelled, or only the growth curve?
  • What is the assumed query concurrency, and what is the urgent query performance under the worst-case concurrent load?
  • How does the model behave if an IoT or fixed-wireless population is added mid-life?
  • What is the re-ingest cost if a classification decision changes and records must be re-derived?

That last one is worth dwelling on. Classification decisions do change, and a store that cannot be re-derived from retained source references means the only options are “leave it wrong” or “lose the history”. Designing for re-derivability is cheap at the start and impossible to retrofit.

Key takeaways

  • The notice is the schema’s requirements baseline; retain what it specifies and nothing more.
  • Correlate on a charging identifier where one exists; never silently join on heuristics; carry a per-source completeness indicator into the disclosure.
  • Separate entity, events and resolution-aid stores physically — different volumes, different periods, different access boundaries.
  • Time partitioning is the deletion mechanism; deletion must produce evidence that outlives the data; crypto-shredding is usually the only real answer to backups.
  • Volume reconciliation against source counts is the control that catches the failure nothing else will.

Two design principles that follow directly.

The authorisation must be machine-readable. Who resolves ambiguity?

The most consequential and least-discussed interface decision. A request may be ambiguous — an identifier that resolves to several candidates, a period that spans a reassignment, a data type that does not exist in this network.

OptionConsequence
CSP resolves and returns its best interpretationFast; but the CSP is making a judgement it has no authority to make, and the authority cannot see it happened

The middle option is nearly always right, and it requires the interface to have a clarification exchange — a first-class message type, not an email. Designs that lack one push the exchange into an unaudited channel, which is where the audit trail breaks.

Governance note. Ask to see the interface specification’s message catalogue. If it has “request” and “response” and nothing else, it will fail in operation and the gaps will be filled by telephone calls and email — with the corresponding loss of audit. A usable catalogue has at least: submit, acknowledge, clarify, amend, withdraw, status, deliver, error, nil-return.

The reconciliation point deserves emphasis.

If both parties keep independent,

comparable records of every exchange, then a discrepancy is detectable.

If only one

does, the other is trusting it. Building reconcilable audit at both ends is the strongest single assurance control available at the seam, and it costs little if designed in.

9.6 Urgent requests

Threat-to-life and similar cases need an answer in minutes, and the design must accommodate that without weakening the controls.

Normal path:  submit -> validate -> queue -> retrieve -> review -> release
              minutes to hours, human review, full assembly

Urgent path:  submit -> validate -> retrieve -> release
              target minutes; pre-agreed narrow scope;
              review AFTER release, not before;
              automatic escalation and 100% post-hoc audit

The design decisions that make the urgent path defensible:

  • Narrow it. The urgent path supports a small, pre-agreed set of request types — typically identity resolution and recent location — not everything.
  • Move the human, do not remove them. Review happens after release, within a defined period, and every urgent disclosure is reviewed, not sampled.
  • Make it visibly different.

Distinct audit stream, distinct reporting, distinct reconciliation with the authority. If urgent volumes rise, that is a governance signal, and it should be visible without anyone running a query.

  • Rehearse it. An urgent path that has never been exercised out of hours will not work at 3 a.m.

Key takeaways

  • The interface must carry machine-readable authorisation attributes, or scope enforcement can only ever be procedural.
  • TS 103 120 for request and authorisation, TS 102 657 for retained-data structures and handover, TS 102 232 for delivery transport, 3GPP 33.127/128 for the network-side architecture.
  • The same architecture carries content and communications data; separation is administrative, so its evidence must be exceptional.
  • Ambiguity belongs with the authority, which means the interface needs a first-class clarification exchange.
  • Reconcilable audit at both ends is the strongest control at the seam; signed requests and responses are what make it work years later.

Module 10 — Security, Compartmentation and Insider Risk [D/G]

Objectives. You can articulate the threat model for a CDA capability; you can design the compartmentation and access model; and you can identify the insider-risk controls that the regime effectively requires even where it does not name them.

10.1 The threat model, stated honestly

A CDA capability is a concentrated, high-value target with an unusual property: its most damaging misuse looks exactly like its intended use. A query run for a personal reason is technically identical to one run for an investigation. That shapes everything.

The CSP’s controls largely assume requests are genuine. Ask what would detect a run of plausible, well-formed, individually unremarkable requests that were not in fact authorised. In

most designs the honest answer is “reconciliation with the authority’s own records, if anyone ever does it” — which is an argument for doing it routinely and automatically.

10.2 Compartmentation

The organising principle: no single person, and no single system component, should be able to go from “I want to know about X” to “here is everything about X”.

Four rules that make this real rather than nominal:

1. Platform engineers must not be able to run disclosure queries. They can keep the

store running; they cannot ask it questions about people. This usually requires the query path to be the only path to plaintext, with platform access limited to encrypted storage and metadata.

2. Disclosure officers must not be able to change the system. No configuration, no

schema, no scope rules.

3. Nobody administers their own audit. The audit compartment is administered by a

separate authority — often internal audit or a security function with no operational role.

4. Privileged actions require two people. Provisioning a target, changing a scope rule,

exporting in bulk, suspending deletion, or granting access.

10.3 Access control that matches the law

Ordinary role-based access control is insufficient here, because the question is not “what may this user do?” but “what may this user do in respect of this authorisation?”

The practical implication: access decisions are per-request, not per-session, and the authorisation is an input to the access decision. A user logged in with a valid role and no live authorisation can do nothing. Accreditation as an attribute. In the UK model, disclosure staff and SPoCs are accredited. Accreditation lapses. Making it a checked attribute rather than an HR record means a lapsed accreditation removes capability automatically, which is both better control and one less thing for an inspection to find.

Preventive controls fail; the regime expects detection too.

10.6 Vendor and supply chain

Three questions that should be settled before contract, not after:

  • Can the vendor reach production, and under what conditions? Standing remote access into a CDA estate is very hard to defend. The alternative — supervised, time-bounded, recorded, break-glass access — is more expensive to operate and far easier to assure.
  • What does the vendor’s software do that you cannot see? Closed components in the query or mediation path mean the assurance case rests on the vendor’s assertions. Source availability, reproducible builds, or at minimum contractual audit rights, change the shape of that case.
  • What happens at end of life? These systems outlive their vendors’ product lines. Data portability and schema ownership are compliance issues, not commercial ones: if you cannot migrate the retained data, you cannot meet the notice with a different supplier.

Key takeaways

  • The defining property of this threat model is that misuse looks exactly like use; that forces detection controls alongside preventive ones.
  • Compartmentation means provisioning, data, disclosure and audit are separately staffed and separately administered, with two-person control on privileged actions.
  • Access control must take the authorisation as an input and decide per request, not per session; accreditation should be a checked attribute.
  • Per-partition keys serve deletion, compartmentation and the proof that platform staff could not read the data.
  • Self-query and associate-query detection is the cheapest high-yield control in the whole design.

Part IV — Assurance and Governance

Module 11 — Quality, Integrity and Evidential Weight [G]

Objectives. You can specify the assurance regime for a CDA capability; you can explain what makes disclosed data evidentially usable and what destroys that; and you can design the error and reporting model the regime expects.

11.1 Three different standards, often confused

StandardQuestion it answersWho needs it
Data qualityIs the data accurate, complete and timely?Everyone, always
Data integrityCan we prove it has not changed since it was created?Anything that may be relied on later
Evidential weightWill a court accept it, and how much will it carry?Only the subset that reaches proceedings

Designs commonly conflate the second and third and then either over-engineer everything or under-engineer the part that matters. The useful discipline is to decide, explicitly, which disclosures are built to evidential standard. In most operators the honest answer is all of them, because you cannot know in advance which will be used — and that answer should be reached deliberately, with its cost accepted, rather than by default.

11.2 What evidential weight actually requires

Not a legal treatment, but the engineering properties that support it:

Provenance. For every disclosed value: which source system produced it, when, through which processing steps, under which version of the transformation logic. “This field came from the S-CSCF record ingested at T under pipeline version 4.2” is a provenance statement; “this came from our retention system” is not.

Integrity. The disclosed data can be shown not to have changed between generation and presentation. Practically: hash at ingest, hash the retained record, hash the disclosure, sign the disclosure, and retain the chain.

Repeatability. The same query against the same data returns the same result. This is harder than it sounds where correlation is involved — a correlated record assembled from late-arriving components may legitimately differ between two runs, which is precisely why the as-at time of the disclosure must be recorded and why the correlated record should be immutable once assembled.

Explicability. A competent witness can explain how the system produced this answer. That is a constraint on design, not on documentation: a pipeline with undocumented heuristics, machine-learned matching, or emergent behaviour produces answers nobody can explain in a witness box. This is the strongest argument against the heuristic correlation warned about in Module 8.

Continuity. The chain of custody from generation to presentation is unbroken and recorded, including every human who handled the data.

Governance note. The explicability constraint is an architectural veto. It rules out design options that would otherwise be attractive — probabilistic identity matching, opaque third-party enrichment, adaptive heuristics. If a vendor proposes any of these in the resolution or correlation path, the question is not “how accurate is it?” but “who will explain it, and what happens when they cannot?”

11.3 The quality framework

Six dimensions, each with a measure, a target and a reporting line. This is the table to put in the assurance strategy.

DimensionMeasureTypical control

Two points that make the difference between a framework and a formality:

  • Measure against expectation, not against itself. A feed that reports its own record count is not a control. The expected count must come from the source side or from an independent model.
  • Report the tail, not the mean. Timeliness at the 50th percentile is irrelevant; what matters is how often a record is not available when an urgent request arrives. Assurance reporting that shows averages is concealing the failures.

11.4 Completeness statements

If Module 6 is right that absence of a record is weak evidence of absence of an event, then the disclosure must say what the system knows about its own completeness.

Disclosure for MSISDN X, period 01-02-2026 to 28-02-2026

Completeness:
  Session records    : complete, no known gaps
  Message records    : feed unavailable 14-02 08:00-16:00 (8h);
                       records for that window may be missing
  Location           : present for 41 of 47 sessions; 6 sessions
                       carried no access-network information
  Correlation        : 45 of 47 fully correlated; 2 partial
                       (interconnect component absent)

That block is more useful to the recipient than a hundred extra records, and it is generatable automatically from the quality controls already described. It is also, if things ever go wrong, the artefact that shows the operator disclosed the limitations of its own data rather than presenting it as complete.

The design requirement: the system must record feed outages, correlation failures and field-level absence at the granularity of the query, so that a completeness statement can be composed for any arbitrary period. Retrofitting this is very hard; designing it in is not.

11.6 The assurance evidence pack

What an inspection or an internal design authority should be able to receive, assembled largely automatically:

1. The data catalogue, with classifications, bases, periods and sign-offs. 2. The mapping from notice to schema, field by field, with gaps recorded. 3. Scope enforcement evidence: for a sample of disclosures, the requested, authorised and

returned field sets and the computed intersection.

4. Quality reporting for the period across the six dimensions. 5. Deletion evidence for every expired partition. 6. Access review: who had which rights, when, and the accreditation status behind them. 7. Misuse detection output, including nil returns — evidence the controls ran. 8. Error register with remediation status. 9. Change record for the period, with capability-impact assessments and any notifications

made.

10. Reconciliation with the acquiring authority’s own records.

Governance note. Ask the programme to produce this pack for a single month, early — ideally before go-live, using test data. What cannot be produced is a gap in the design, and finding it at that point costs a fraction of finding it at the first inspection. It is the single most useful thing a design authority can demand.

Key takeaways

  • Quality, integrity and evidential weight are three different standards; decide explicitly which disclosures need which.
  • Evidential weight needs provenance, integrity, repeatability, explicability and continuity — and explicability is an architectural veto on opaque matching.
  • Measure quality against independent expectation, and report the tail.
  • Completeness statements that travel with the disclosure are cheap to generate and disproportionately valuable.
  • Design the error taxonomy with the remediation path included: detecting an error you cannot trace to affected disclosures is not enough.
  • Rehearse the assurance evidence pack before go-live.

Module 12 — Governing the Programme [G]

Objectives. You can set up and run the design authority for a CDA programme; you can structure requirements that derive from a legal instrument; you can manage vendors and change in a regulated capability; and you can recognise the failure patterns early.

12.1 What is different about governing this

Five things, and each has a structural consequence.

The requirements baseline is a legal instrument.

A notice is not a requirements

document: it is written to be legally effective, not implementable. Somebody must translate it, and that translation is itself a governed artefact that must be re-validated when the notice changes.

The customer is not the user. The acquiring authority sets the demand; your own disclosure team uses the system; the Secretary of State compels the capability; the regulator inspects it; and none of them is the one funding it. Stakeholder management is not a soft skill here, it is the job.

Failure is asymmetric. Under-delivering is a compliance breach. Over-delivering — retaining more, returning more, building more capability than is required — is also a breach, and a more serious one in privacy terms. Programmes are instinctively built to over-deliver.

The evidence is part of the deliverable. A capability that works but cannot prove it worked is not done.

The lifetime is long and the sources churn. A retention capability outlives the network generation that fed it. Modularity is a compliance property.

12.2 The design authority

Membership. Enough to make the decision, small enough to make it. In practice: a chair with design authority; the solution architect; a security architect; someone who owns the legal interpretation; someone who owns the operational disclosure function; and data/privacy. The last two are the ones most often missing, and their absence shows up as designs that are elegant and unoperable, or compliant on paper and intrusive in practice.

12.7 A first hundred days

If you are picking this up cold, the sequence that recovers ground fastest:

1. Get the instruments. The notices, the current Code, the interpretation record. Read

them before the architecture.

2. Build the field-level catalogue, even if it starts as an incomplete spreadsheet, and find

out who has signed off the arguable classifications. Usually nobody has.

3. Trace one real disclosure end to end — request to response, with the audit — and see

whether you can reconstruct it. This exposes more than a month of document review.

4. Ask for last month’s expected-versus-actual volumes per feed. If nobody can

produce them, you have found the most serious operational gap.

5. Run the assurance pack rehearsal (Module 11) on test data. 6. Establish the decision list in §12.2 and find out which of those decisions have actually

been taken, by whom, and when.

7. Write the capability statement and get it agreed with the acquiring authority.

Key takeaways

  • The requirements baseline is a legal instrument and must be translated through a versioned interpretation chain; requirements never cite the law directly.
  • Over-delivery is a breach too — record the negative decisions as evidence of minimisation.
  • Buy the plumbing, own the policy: classification, scope, correlation, caveats and audit are your compliance posture and must be portable.
  • A mandatory capability-impact question on every change is the operational mechanism behind both notification obligations and regression control.
  • The fastest diagnostic in an unfamiliar programme is to trace one disclosure end to end and ask for last month’s expected-versus-actual feed volumes.

Appendix B — Patterns and Anti-Patterns

B.1 Patterns worth adopting

Catalogue as a build artefact. The field catalogue — classification, basis, period, source, owner — is machine-readable and consumed by the pipeline and the scope resolver. Schema and catalogue cannot drift because the catalogue generates the enforcement.

Interval-keyed bindings. Every association is (identifier, type, from, to). Every resolution takes a time and returns segments with explicit gaps. This one pattern prevents the largest class of wrongful disclosure.

Scope resolver as a service.

A component beneath the application intersects the

authorisation with the request and emits a permitted field set and period. Every query goes through it; the intersection is logged. Converts “we trained our staff” into “the system could not have.”

Truncate at ingest, irreversibly. The content-bearing value never reaches storage. One engineering decision replaces a permanent operational risk.

Per-partition keys. Time partitions hold their own keys; expiry destroys the key. Deletion becomes provable and reaches backups.

Completeness statement as a first-class output. Feed outages, correlation failures and field absence are recorded at a granularity that lets a statement be composed for any query period, and it travels with the disclosure.

Two pipelines, one policy. Urgent and routine paths are physically distinct, with different latency targets and different review placement, but share the same scope resolver and the same audit.

Reconcilable audit at both ends. Both parties record every exchange in comparable form, and reconciliation runs automatically rather than on suspicion.

Negative decision register. What was deliberately not built, and why, kept as evidence of minimisation.

B.3 Two worked design decisions

Where to enforce scope

Context. Requests arrive with an authorisation reference. The authorisation permits certain data classes for a certain period.

Options. (a) Disclosure officers apply it. (b) The application restricts the form. (c) A scope-resolution service intersects authorisation and request and returns a permitted set that the query layer enforces. (d) Row and column security in the data layer bound to the authorisation.

Decision. (c), with (d) for the highest-sensitivity classes.

Rationale. (a) and (b) cannot produce the evidence an inspection needs and fail under volume. (d) alone is inflexible and slow to change as notices change. (c) puts the intersection in one auditable place, keeps the policy in configuration we own, and produces the requested/authorised/returned triple that the assurance pack depends on.

Consequences. Every request must carry machine-readable authorisation attributes — this becomes an interface requirement. The scope resolver is a high-assurance component with its own change control.

Retention source for voice session records

Context. Charging records already exist and are complete for billable events. The notice specifies session records including unanswered attempts.

Options. (a) Reuse charging. (b) Purpose-built collection from the S-CSCF and P-CSCF. (c) Charging now, purpose-built later.

Decision. (c), with a dated commitment and a published gap list.

Rationale. (a) cannot meet the notice — unanswered attempts are not billable — and couples compliance to the tariff roadmap. (b) is right but not deliverable in the required timescale. (c) delivers a partial capability honestly, provided the gaps are declared to the authority rather than concealed.

Consequences. A published gap list; a change-coupling statement naming who is consulted before billing changes; a capability statement update; and a date at which (c) becomes (b) or is re-decided.
